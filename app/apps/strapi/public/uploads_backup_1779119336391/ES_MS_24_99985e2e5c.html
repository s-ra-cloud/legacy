Introduction

The originality of this article lies not so much in its methodology as in the singularity of its subject and the empirical material on which it is based. While the processes leading to jihadist violence have been the focus of extensive research—particularly within the French-speaking academic sphere over the past decade—similar trajectories within the universe of radical far-right extremism remain largely underexplored. Existing literature has primarily focused on legalist far-right movements—political parties, associations, electoral strategies, and ideological dynamics—yet few studies have investigated the biographical pathways that lead certain individuals to adopt illegal, and even terrorist, forms of violence. This scarcity can be explained by several factors. On the one hand, the clandestine nature of violent engagement makes individual trajectories difficult to access. Militants preparing for violent action typically operate outside public view, while those who have already crossed that threshold are often incarcerated and reluctant to share their stories in a research setting. On the other hand, far-right terrorism remains marginal in the French prison system, with only a few dozen inmates among a total prison population of nearly 80,000. These characteristics significantly limit access to rich empirical data necessary for in-depth sociological analysis.

It is precisely in this context of rarity that the present case study situates itself. Through an analysis of the trajectory of Kylian—founder of the small OAS group (Organisation des Armées Sociales), arrested in 2017 while preparing attacks against targets identified as “enemies of the nation”—this article aims to contribute to a comprehensive sociology of far-right radicalization in France. Far from a sensationalist account or a purely factual narrative, the purpose here is to reconstruct the gradual mechanisms of subjective transformation at work in his trajectory, shedding light on the social, emotional, and digital dynamics that underlie adherence to a violent political project. Nisin’s trajectory, in its specificity, reveals a sequence of structuring emotional phases—humiliation, emulation, exaltation, anger, vengeance—that allow us to read his commitment as a sequential and affective process of radicalization. It shows how early experiences of social exclusion can foster a victim-centered perception of the world, how digital platforms offer spaces for self-revalorization and recognition, and how the construction of a militant self can culminate in an intimate legitimization of violence. This article also aims to explore the pertinence of a comparative approach between far-right and jihadist radicalizations. While the ideological foundations of these two universes appear radically opposed, a closer sociological reading reveals significant structural convergences.

This study adopts a sociobiographical approach, drawing on the work of Norbert Elias on habitus formation, Danilo Martuccelli on social trials, and Farhad Khosrokhavar and Jérôme Ferret on contemporary radicalization. It also incorporates insights from the sociology of emotions (Scheff, Collins, Ferret) and digital communication studies (Cardon, Douzet, Tufekci), particularly around the notion of digital extimacy—the public staging of the self in spaces that are both intimate and political. The analysis is further informed by the concept of cognitive insularity, linked to repeated algorithmic exposure to ideologically homogeneous content, which reinforces radical beliefs and deactivates critical capacities. Empirically, the analysis is based on a hybrid set of materials: judicial sources (investigative files, trial transcripts), online publications (social media, forums, videos), and digital monitoring of the virtual spaces frequented by Kylian and his associates. Though fragmentary and sometimes ambiguous, these materials make it possible to retrace the key stages of a radicalization process in which emotion and the search for recognition play a central role.

Contrary to simplistic readings based on sudden shifts or ideological contagion, we offer here a processual, emotional, and digital interpretation of radical engagement. We hypothesize that the case of Kylian paradigmatically illustrates a type of commitment in which digital technologies, socialization wounds, and emotional dynamics interact to produce a militant subjectivity in rupture with the social order. It is through the sequential reconstruction of this trajectory that the following section seeks to uncover the inner workings of this emo-digital dynamic, showing how an isolated adolescent gradually becomes an activist convinced of the legitimacy of a violent political project.

Implicitly, this study also raises the question of the possibility of a metanoia—that is, an existential reversal or a moral reconfiguration occurring after radical engagement. The concept, often associated with religious conversion or a profound subjective rupture, allows us to conceive of radicalization as a potentially reversible process, albeit one that requires a significant narrative break and a critical reworking of self-representations. While the case of Nisin does not allow us to draw definitive conclusions regarding the plausibility of such a transformation, it nonetheless provides a valuable observational ground for examining the conditions under which this metanoia might emerge—or, conversely, be obstructed—within radicalization trajectories.

1. An Emo-Digital Trajectory of Radicalization: From Humiliation to Vengeance

Kylian’s trajectory is marked by a biographical progression shaped by the entanglement of personal vulnerabilities, early ideological socialization, and intense digital investment. Far from resulting from a sudden shift, his engagement unfolds through a gradual dynamic of radicalization, structured around a succession of emotional states that can be grouped into five phases: humiliation, emulation, exaltation, anger, and vengeance. Each of these emotional stages is intimately tied to digital sociability and a search for identity reassurance.

1.1. Foundational Humiliation: Stigmatization, Isolation, and the Emergence of Racialized Resentment

The first structuring moment in Kylian’s trajectory is rooted in a lasting experience of social humiliation. Subjected to repeated mockery due to facial tics, he endured various episodes of school bullying across institutions in the Marseille area and Vitrolles. This ordinary violence, taking place in environments where students were predominantly of immigrant backgrounds, gradually fostered in him a sense of injustice and persecution. The experience of shame, exclusion, and rejection—at an age when peer recognition is vital—crystallized into a form of resentment, in the Nietzschean sense: an internalized anger without outlet, searching for a target. From this wound emerges a worldview shaped by the notion of identity-based antagonism. School is no longer perceived as a space of integration, but as a hostile territory where the humiliation suffered is attributed to symbolic and racial domination by populations seen as "foreign." This racialized interpretation of suffering forms an emotional foundation upon which ideological justifications are later constructed. Kylian’s trajectory demonstrates how personal wounds can be politically reconfigured, transforming feelings of individual inferiority into a collective victimhood narrative.

1.2. Digital Refuge and Radical Emulation: Extimacy, Community, and Compensation

Confronted with school isolation and status loss, Kylian gradually withdraws from the ordinary social world and retreats into digital spaces. He discovers in identity-focused forums, radical Facebook groups, and encrypted messaging platforms a set of alternative sociabilities that will play a decisive role in restoring his self-esteem. These spaces operate as zones of extimacy (Tisseron), where one can project one’s interiority while submitting it to the validation of an ideologically homogeneous circle.  Within these micro-digital communities, he finds not only a sympathetic ear, but also validation: his narratives of suffering, his racist views, his paranoid historical interpretations are no longer marginalized but affirmed, encouraged, and aestheticized. This validation produces a powerful sense of emulation. The previously isolated youth becomes a “comrade,” a member of a supposed vanguard lucid about civilizational decline. He begins interacting with militants and sympathizers from ultra-nationalist, royalist, or neo-fascist groups, with whom he shares a common language, historical references (OAS, the Crusades, Vichy France), and a virile aesthetic of confrontation. In this universe, ideological investment becomes a medium for self-construction. He identifies with a chosen community—those who “see clearly”—and assumes a political role: that of the watchman, the whistleblower, the resister. This online politicization process is inseparable from an affective dynamic of narcissistic repair: it enables him to transform a lived experience of marginalization into a source of symbolic distinction.

1.3. Digital Exaltation: Militant Prestige and the Illusion of Mastery

As his involvement deepens, Kylian evolves from a mere participant to a network organizer. He creates content, amplifies calls to action, and positions himself as an influencer within identitarian spheres. This new role grants him unprecedented prestige, magnified by the virality of social platforms. Behind the screen, he develops a form of digital charisma, rallying a dispersed but loyal audience and giving his actions a quasi-national reach. This online recognition acts as an identity revelator: the socially excluded student becomes a respected militant, whose voice matters and whose positions carry authority. Radicalization ceases to be a refuge and becomes a mission. The resulting exaltation is not only ideological but existential—it gives meaning to a life previously marked by rejection.

This moment of exaltation is also reinforced by algorithmic mechanisms specific to platforms, which reinforce existing beliefs by exposing users to increasingly homogeneous ideological content. The cognitive bubble effect gradually isolates Kylian from dissenting perspectives, while giving him the illusion of possessing hidden truth.

1.4. Anger as a Turning Point: The November 2015 Shock

The November 13, 2015 terrorist attacks in Paris constitute a rupture in his trajectory—a moral shock, as conceptualized by James Jasper, meaning an event perceived as a direct violation of one’s core values. The attacks are experienced by Kylian as the absolute confirmation of his ideological diagnosis: France is at war, the enemies are known, the urgency is total. The anger that follows is not merely emotional—it becomes politically articulated. It provides a legitimizing framework for crossing a new threshold: that of justified violence. From that point on, his rhetoric radicalizes, his language militarizes, and his stance transforms: no longer to denounce, but to act, to resist, to strike.

1.5. Vengeance as Horizon: Toward a Sacrificial Militant Subjectivity

The final stage of this trajectory sees the desire for vengeance take a structured and operational form. Nisin now views himself as a tragic hero, a would-be resister, a soldier of national salvation. This sacrificial self-identification—nourished by far-right mythologies (OAS, wartime resistance, identitarian martyrs)—pushes him toward violent actions targeting designated enemies of the nation (journalists, Muslims, antifascist activists, etc.). Violence is not conceived as a desperate act, but as a culmination. It marks the completion of an identity construction, in which the marginalized individual becomes the agent of collective redemption. At this stage, the initial negative affects (shame, loneliness, humiliation) are entirely reworked into a posture of strength and legitimacy.

Comparative Radicalizations: Parallels Between Far-Right and Jihadist Trajectories

Kylian’s trajectory, in its emotional, digital, and biographical dimensions, mirrors patterns frequently identified in jihadist commitments. Both involve disqualified life paths, the digital reconfiguration of identity, and the construction of a heroic self-legitimated by violence. By analyzing these parallels, we seek to move beyond ideological compartmentalization and propose a transversal framework for understanding contemporary violent engagements. This comparative lens allows us to consider radicalization not merely as an adherence to a belief system, but as an affective and narrative response to perceived dislocation and marginality.

If the preceding section has reconstructed the emotional and digital dynamics specific to Kylian’s trajectory, it now becomes possible to extend the analytical framework beyond this singular case by comparing it with patterns identified in jihadist radicalizations. Such a comparison does not aim to erase ideological differences or minimize the specificities of each universe, but to uncover structural homologies that traverse different radical milieus. What connects certain far-right and jihadist pathways is not a shared doctrine, but a set of biographical configurations, affective sequences, and digital mechanisms that shape militant subjectivities in similar ways.

2.1. Disqualified Lives and Biographical Dissonance

In both jihadist and far-right trajectories, we frequently observe the experience of biographical dissonance: a gap between the subjective expectations of recognition and the objective possibilities of integration. Young individuals, often from modest or fractured backgrounds, feel socially humiliated, culturally disoriented, or morally devalued. This dissonance generates a feeling of being out of place in the social world, a perception of marginalization that becomes fertile ground for ideological reinterpretation.

In Kylian’s case, school bullying and status loss catalyzed a process of racialized resentment. In jihadist cases, similar patterns have been observed—social discrimination, stigmatization of religious practices, or postcolonial ressentiment—leading to a politicization of suffering. Whether framed through the lenses of identity (far-right) or umma (jihadism), the emotional wound is reconfigured into a moral indictment of society. In both instances, personal trauma is recoded as collective injustice.

2.2. Digital Echo Chambers and Algorithmic Captivity

The digital sphere functions as an emotional and ideological incubator for both types of radicalization. Individuals retreat into algorithmically reinforced spaces where they encounter confirmation, validation, and identity support. These online environments enable the formation of micro-communities of belief, where affective investments and political convictions reinforce one another in loops of mutual excitation.

Whether it is jihadist forums extolling martyrdom or far-right threads celebrating nationalist resistance, the structure remains the same: a digitally mediated collective intimacy in which the isolated subject becomes visible, valuable, and valorized. As in Kylian’s case, the staging of the self through memes, symbols, and declarations functions as a narcissistic repair mechanism, transforming social invisibility into symbolic distinction. The digital also plays a key role in desensitizing individuals to violence and fostering operational emulation.

2.3. Heroization and Sacrificial Narratives

Another striking parallel lies in the desire for heroization. In both jihadist and far-right imaginaries, violence is not only justified—it is sanctified. The militant subject aspires to transcend ordinariness through a sacrificial act that gives meaning to life and death. The jihadist mujahid and the far-right “resister” are both shaped by mythologies of redemption in which violence becomes the language of moral clarity.

Kylian’s self-identification as a “soldier of the nation,” his references to the OAS and to a fantasized tradition of patriotic violence, mirror the jihadist recourse to historical caliphates and Qur’anic heroism. In both configurations, the subject seeks to inscribe his suffering within a grand historical narrative, where the act of violence represents both culmination and transcendence.

2.4. The Closed Circuit of Recognition

At the heart of these processes lies a quest for recognition, structured through peer validation, group belonging, and self-legitimation. In both domains, the militant group functions as a moral community, offering not only ideological coherence but emotional safety. The passage to violence is rarely the product of solitary conviction; it is a collectively supported performance, enacted within an echo chamber that provides justification, encouragement, and applause.

This dynamic is particularly visible in both jihadist cells and far-right micro-groups, where ritualization, secrecy, and loyalty become structuring elements. What is shared here is less a doctrine than a form of commitment: intense, intimate, and staged as destiny.

2.5. Toward a Comparative Sociology of Radicalization

These structural parallels invite us to de-ideologize the analysis of radicalization. Without dissolving the importance of belief systems, a focus on emotions, narratives, digital infrastructures, and social recognition provides a transversal lens through which to examine different forms of violent engagement. The case of Kylian thus serves not only as a sociological entry point into the world of far-right radicalism but also as a comparative bridge, highlighting the need to think across ideological lines. It suggests that radicalization is less the adoption of a specific doctrine than the resolution of a biographical and emotional dissonance through action—a logic that transcends political boundaries.

This comparative perspective also challenges prevention policies that treat each radical threat in isolation. If the same mechanisms of hurt, validation, and heroization recur in different ideological settings, then counter-radicalization must be sensitive to subjective experience, and capable of intercepting not just content, but the emotional grammars and digital architectures through which individuals come to see violence as meaningful.

Conclusion: A Militant Subjectivity Shaped by Emotion and the Digital

Kylian’s trajectory reveals the complex mechanics of far-right radicalization, which arise neither from raw ideological indoctrination nor from a purely biographical accident, but from a gradual entanglement of emotional vulnerabilities, identity reconfigurations, and digital sociabilities. The path from humiliation to vengeance—analyzed here through five affective sequences—illuminates how a militant subjectivity can emerge, one that sees violence as a legitimate political horizon. What this case reveals above all is the cumulative and nonlinear nature of radicalization: each emotional phase does not erase the previous one, but rather accumulates, reactivates, and reframes itself within new interactional contexts. Shame becomes inverted pride, loneliness transforms into communal exaltation, anger becomes moral justification. The digital plays a central role here—not only as a vector for ideological socialization, but also as a space of repair and recognition, where wounded subjectivities find ways to reassemble themselves. In this sense, Kylian embodies a form of radicalization specific to contemporary societies: individualized, affective, screen-mediated, and built on composite ideological fragments rather than a rigid doctrinal foundation. He is not so much a fanatic as a young man whose social wounds were progressively converted into a mission narrative, within a closed universe where violence appears coherent and valorized.

Far from any deterministic interpretation, this analysis underscores the importance of taking affects, digital mediations, and recognition processes seriously when studying contemporary violent engagements. It calls for a sensitive, longitudinal approach to radical trajectories—one that pays attention to how individuals reinvest their own suffering into militant narratives, and how these narratives can, in certain cases, lead to violent action.
